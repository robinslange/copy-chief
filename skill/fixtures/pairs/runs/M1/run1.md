**Gate 0: CLEAR.**
- The only named organisation is the seller itself, and "Grey Lynn" is where the seller works.
- Price, the Tuesday-to-Friday turnaround and the refund/redo terms are the seller's own commercial terms, which are exempt.
- "sharp enough to slice a tomato without pressing" is a first-party performance claim, not a third-party fact about the world. A buyer can check it the first time they cut, so it can't be faked without anyone noticing. It is handled below under H3, P3 and X2, not blocked.

**Findings, most severe first**

1. **H1 (High).** "# Introducing Grey Lynn Knife Co."
   What fails: with the rest of the page covered, all you learn from this headline is that a business exists and is new. It promises nothing specific and leaves no specific question open. It is word for word the failure pattern in the check ("Introducing our new range").
   Principle: Caples and Ogilvy, a headline carries a specific promise or opens a specific gap.

2. **H4 (High).** First screen: "Introducing Grey Lynn Knife Co." / "Grey Lynn Knife Co. offers professional whetstone sharpening services."
   What fails: the hook the draft actually has (the tomato test) first appears in the third sentence, below the part of the page a first-time visitor sees first. The hook arrives after the first unit.
   Principle: the medium's scroll-stop physics, where the hook's work has to happen inside the H1 and first screen.

3. **H2 (High).** "Grey Lynn Knife Co. offers professional whetstone sharpening services."
   What fails: the sentence is complete in itself. Company, what it does and how are all stated, with no timeframe, condition or missing half to pull the reader into the next sentence. It also repeats the headline's name.
   Principle: Sugarman's slippery slide, the first sentence's only job is to get the second one read.

4. **M1 (Medium).** Opens with "Introducing Grey Lynn Knife Co." and the company name again in the next sentence.
   What fails: opening on the business name assumes a product-aware reader. A first-time visitor more likely arrives problem-aware: their knife is dull and squashes a tomato. The draft never names that problem. It only implies it, through the tomato line, two sentences later.
   Principle: Schwartz's awareness states, the copy should address the stage the reader is actually in.

5. **H3 (Medium).** "sharp enough to slice a tomato without pressing" and "professional whetstone sharpening services".
   What fails:
   - The tomato promise is the main hook, but the guarantee pays out on a different standard: "If a knife is not sharper than the day you bought it". A knife could pass that test and still fail the tomato test, and the reader has no remedy tied to the promise they were actually sold.
   - "professional" is never returned to. There is no training, years of practice or grit progression to back it.
   Principle: Hopkins, no hook claim the body fails to pay off, tracked claim by claim.

6. **C2 (Medium).** "Drop your knives in the box at the shop door by Tuesday" and "Book a drop-off at knives.example/drop … You get a text with your box number within the hour."
   What fails: doing what the page says still leaves the next step unclear.
   - The page never says what is at the link (a form? payment?).
   - It never says when or how the reader pays.
   - It never says where "the shop door" is beyond the suburb.
   - The body tells the reader to drop knives in "the box" as if they could do that straight away, but the close says a box number only arrives after booking. A reader following the body could turn up without having booked.
   Principle: Kennedy, instructions unambiguous: where the click lands, what arrives, and when.

7. **P3 (Medium).** "I sharpen kitchen knives by hand on whetstones"
   What fails: asked "why does this work?", the draft gives the method's name and no chain. It never says what hand sharpening on whetstones does to the edge that a pull-through or machine sharpener does not, or why that produces the tomato result. It also leaves unsupported the implied claim in the guarantee that the result beats a factory edge.
   Principle: the Schwartz line on the unique mechanism, a claim needs a because.

8. **V2 (Low).** "Grey Lynn Knife Co. offers professional whetstone sharpening services." followed by "I sharpen kitchen knives…"
   What fails: the opening talks about the business in the corporate third person and addresses nobody. The voice then switches to a first-person tradesperson speaking to "you". The first two lines don't reach the reader.
   Principle: Halbert, one-to-one address.

9. **O4 (Low).** "Drop your knives in the box at the shop door by Tuesday and they are back on Friday"
   What fails: this is the second direction of the check. The draft already has a real time-bound fact, a weekly Tuesday cut-off, but never ties it to acting now. For example, it never says that booking today gets the knives into this week's batch and missing Tuesday means waiting a week. There is no invented urgency, just a real reason that goes unused.
   Principle: Kennedy and Cialdini, use a real reason to act now when the draft already holds one.

10. **X2 (Low).** "they are back on Friday, sharp enough to slice a tomato without pressing" and "NZ$18 a knife, any length."
    What fails: the Friday return is a fulfilment term and is fine. The tomato result is a different kind of statement: an unhedged performance outcome promised for every knife, whatever its steel or condition. The guarantee does not cover that outcome (see H3). Under NZ's Fair Trading Act (the counterpart to the FTC doctrine the check cites), a representation about how something will perform has to be one the seller can back when it's made.
    Principle: no implied outcome guarantee beyond what can be substantiated.

11. **X1 (Low; this goes beyond the check's body-claim test).** "bring it back within a week and I redo it free or refund you"
    What fails: there are no body claims, so the literal X1 test passes. On jurisdictional posture, though, a service sold to NZ consumers comes with Consumer Guarantees Act rights (reasonable care and skill) that a trader cannot contract out of. A bare one-week window could be read as limiting those rights, and a Fair Trading Act representation risk sits there. The page doesn't say the guarantee is in addition to statutory rights.
    Principle: claim posture matches jurisdiction.
