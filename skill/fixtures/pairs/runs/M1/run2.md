Findings, most severe first:

1. H1 (high). "Introducing Grey Lynn Knife Co."
The headline says only that something new exists and what it is called. With the rest of the page covered, you cannot tell what result it promises or what question it opens. That is the "Introducing our new range" failure almost word for word. Principle: Caples and Ogilvy, a headline must carry a specific promise or open a specific gap.

2. H4 (high). The first screen: "Introducing Grey Lynn Knife Co." / "Grey Lynn Knife Co. offers professional whetstone sharpening services."
On a landing page the first thing the reader meets is the H1 plus the first screen. Neither holds a hook. The only hook on the page, the tomato line, sits in the second paragraph, after the reader has decided whether to stay. Principle: the hook has to do its work inside the first unit the medium gives it.

3. H2 (high). "Grey Lynn Knife Co. offers professional whetstone sharpening services."
This first body sentence completes its own thought: who, what, done. It leaves nothing unfinished to pull the reader on. It also repeats the headline's name, and it speaks in a corporate third person that the next sentence drops for "I". Principle: Sugarman's slippery slide, the first sentence exists only to get the second one read.

4. M1 (medium). "Introducing Grey Lynn Knife Co." / "Grey Lynn Knife Co. offers..."
The draft opens with the business name, so it assumes a reader who already knows the product. A first-time visitor more likely arrives with a problem, a knife that crushes a tomato, and no idea this service exists. That problem is never named. The closest the page gets is the outcome in paragraph two. Principle: Schwartz's awareness stages, copy must meet the reader at the stage they are actually in.

5. H3 (medium). Two early claims are never paid off.
(a) "professional". Nothing later backs it: no years, no training, no count of knives done. It is never mentioned again.
(b) "sharp enough to slice a tomato without pressing". Nothing explains how this is achieved or shows it happening. The guarantee does not cover it either, because it pays out against a different and weaker standard: "If a knife is not sharper than the day you bought it". A knife can pass that test and still fail the tomato. Principle: Hopkins, every promise the hook makes must be paid off in the body.

6. X2 (medium). "they are back on Friday, sharp enough to slice a tomato without pressing"
"back on Friday" is a delivery term and does not count. "sharp enough to slice a tomato without pressing" is a flat promise about the result, and nothing on the page supports it. Knife steels, damage and blade shapes vary, and the guarantee does not reach this standard (see H3). The guarantee's own test, "sharper than the day you bought it", has no stated judge or measure, so it is unclear when it triggers. In New Zealand, unsupported claims about how well a service performs are exposed under the Fair Trading Act. Principle: an outcome you promise has to be one you can back up before you make it.

7. C2 (medium). "Drop your knives in the box at the shop door by Tuesday" and "Book a drop-off at knives.example/drop and quote code TOMATO. You get a text with your box number within the hour."
The page gives two orders of events that do not fit together. Paragraph two tells the reader to walk up and drop the knives in the box. The call to action says to book first and wait for a box number. The page also never says where "the shop door" is (no address), or when and how the customer pays. From the page alone, the reader cannot tell where to go after the text or when money changes hands. Principle: Kennedy, the next step must be stated without ambiguity.

8. P3 (medium). "I sharpen kitchen knives by hand on whetstones"
Asked why the knife comes back tomato-sharp, the page's best answer is "by hand on whetstones". That names a method, but not the chain of cause and effect. Nothing says what the stone or the hand does that a pull-through sharpener or a supermarket service does not, or why that produces the result. Principle: the Schwartz lineage, name the mechanism, give the "because".

9. O4 (low). "by Tuesday and they are back on Friday"
The page states a weekly cutoff: miss Tuesday and you wait another week. It never ties that to acting now, for example by saying to book today so the knives are back this Friday. The reason to act now is already on the page and goes unused. This is the second way O4 can fail, a real reason left unused, not invented urgency. Principle: Kennedy and Cialdini, a real reason to act now.
