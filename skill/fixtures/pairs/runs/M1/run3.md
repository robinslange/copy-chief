VERDICT: PASS-WITH-FINDINGS

CLOSING UNIT: Yes, this is the closing unit. The medium is a landing page, and the draft states a price ("NZ$18 a knife"), a guarantee and a direct booking action ("Book a drop-off at knives.example/drop"). O1, O3, O4 and C3 all run.

GATE 0: CLEAR. Every number and term is first-party: the prices, the Tuesday-to-Friday turnaround, the one-week redo-or-refund term, the code TOMATO and the text "within the hour". "Grey Lynn Knife Co." is the seller and "Grey Lynn" is where the seller works. The draft cites no statistic, study, outside person, outside organisation or outside date. "Sharp enough to slice a tomato without pressing" is the seller describing their own work, and a buyer can test it on the first tomato. It can't be faked silently the way a citation can, so it is handled under H3 below and not here.

FINDINGS (most severe first)

1. H1 (high). Quoted line: "# Introducing Grey Lynn Knife Co."
   What fails: With the rest of the page covered, the headline tells you only that a business exists and is new. It makes no promise and leaves no question open. The copy's strongest line is the tomato test, and it is kept out of the headline.
   Principle: Caples and Ogilvy say a headline must carry a specific promise or open a specific gap. "Introducing our new range" is the example of failure named in the rubric, and this headline has that shape.

2. M1 (high). Quoted lines: "# Introducing Grey Lynn Knife Co." / "Grey Lynn Knife Co. offers professional whetstone sharpening services."
   What fails: The page opens with the business name twice, so it assumes the reader already knows the product. A first-time visitor is most likely only problem-aware: their knives are blunt, and they squash tomatoes instead of slicing them. The draft never names that problem. The reader's own experience only shows up indirectly, as the result in paragraph two.
   Principle: Schwartz's awareness stages. The copy has to meet the reader in the state they arrive in.

3. H2 (medium). Quoted line: "Grey Lynn Knife Co. offers professional whetstone sharpening services."
   What fails: The first sentence of the body answers its own subject (who they are and what they sell), so nothing pulls the reader on. The next sentence then says the same thing again in the first person ("I sharpen kitchen knives by hand on whetstones in Grey Lynn"). The switch from third-person company voice to "I" also shows the first sentence doing no work.
   Principle: Sugarman's slippery slide. The first sentence exists only to get the second one read.

4. H3 (medium). Quoted line: "they are back on Friday, sharp enough to slice a tomato without pressing."
   What fails: This is the draft's one bold promise about a result, and nothing later pays it off. The guarantee sets a different and lower bar: "If a knife is not sharper than the day you bought it". That bar is also one the buyer can't measure, because they no longer know how sharp the knife was on the day they bought it. A knife could fail the tomato test, pass the guarantee, and not be covered.
   Principle: Hopkins. Every claim made up front needs a later sentence that backs it, and the guarantee has to cover the promise the page actually makes.

5. C2 (medium). Quoted lines: "Drop your knives in the box at the shop door by Tuesday" / "Book a drop-off at knives.example/drop and quote code TOMATO. You get a text with your box number within the hour."
   What fails: Following the call to action, the reader lands at knives.example/drop and gets a text with a box number within the hour. Then they hit a gap. The page never says where "the shop door" is (no street address), how or when payment happens, or where to collect on Friday. The order of steps is also unclear. Paragraph two tells the reader to drop knives in "the box" with no mention of booking, and only the close shows that booking comes first and assigns the box.
   Principle: Kennedy. The reader must be able to say what happens next, where, and when, from the page alone.

6. O4 (low; the second direction, a reason the draft already holds and doesn't use). Quoted line: "Drop your knives in the box at the shop door by Tuesday and they are back on Friday"
   What fails: The draft states a real weekly cutoff (Tuesday in, Friday back) and never connects it to acting now. Nothing says that missing Tuesday means waiting another week. The numbered boxes also suggest limited capacity, which the draft doesn't use. The Tuesday deadline also has no reason given for it.
   Principle: Kennedy and Cialdini on scarcity. Use a real time-bound reason when you have one.

7. P1 (low). Quoted line: "offers professional whetstone sharpening services."
   What fails: "Professional" and "services" could go unchanged into any other trade's copy. Everything else on the page is specific ("by hand", "NZ$18", "Friday", "tomato"), so this is the one line that could belong to anyone. More specific material was available and appears one sentence later.
   Principle: Hopkins. Specific over general.

Checked and not fired:
- M2: the tomato test plus the hand-and-whetstone method go beyond a bare claim.
- M3 and V2: one local home cook, addressed as a singular "you".
- M4: the tomato result catches a want the reader already had.
- O1: "I get kitchen knives sharpened by hand on whetstones, back on Friday, for NZ$18 a knife."
- O2: outcome, time, effort and guarantee are all present.
- O3: redo free or refund within a week.
- H4: the tomato line falls within the first screen of a page this short, even though the headline doesn't carry it.
- P2: no comparative argument is made.
- P3: the method-to-result chain is thin but present.
- P4: no appeal to authority.
- V1: concrete apart from item 7.
- V3: sentence lengths range from about 4 to 28 words.
- V4: no dashes, no listed words or constructions.
- C1: one ask.
- C3: code TOMATO plus a dedicated URL.
- X1: no claims about effects on the body.
- X2: "back on Friday" is a promise about what the seller will do, not an outcome guarantee. The tomato claim describes the knife, not the reader, and is dealt with under H3.
