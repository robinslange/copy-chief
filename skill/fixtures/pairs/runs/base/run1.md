## Findings, most severe first

**1. C2: the instructions are unclear (medium)**
- Quoted lines: "Drop your knives in the box at the shop door by Tuesday" and "Book a drop-off at knives.example/drop and quote code TOMATO. You get a text with your box number within the hour."
- **What fails:**
  - The two lines give two different processes. The first says to drop the knives in "the box", as if there is one box and no booking. The second says to book first and wait to be given a numbered box. From the page alone, the reader can't tell whether booking is required before dropping off, or whether they can just walk up.
  - The page never gives an address. "The shop door … in Grey Lynn" names a suburb, not a door, so the reader can't carry out the physical step the booking leads to.
  - It never says when or how the reader pays.
- **Principle:** Kennedy. The reader should be able to follow the call to action and describe every next step from the page. The click and the text are clear; the steps after them are not.

**2. O4: a real reason to act now goes unused (low)**
- Quoted line: "Drop your knives in the box at the shop door by Tuesday and they are back on Friday"
- **What fails:** The draft has a real time limit, the Tuesday cutoff, but never uses it as a reason to act now. It doesn't say what happens to knives dropped after Tuesday, for example that they wait for next week's batch and the reader goes another week without a sharp knife. The cutoff reads as a plain schedule rather than a reason to book today.
- **Principle:** Kennedy; Cialdini on scarcity. When a draft already holds a real reason to act now and leaves it unused, that is a failure.

**3. X1: the claim may not suit New Zealand (low; I could not verify the law, so check with an adviser)**
- Quoted line: "If a knife is not sharper than the day you bought it, bring it back within a week and I redo it free or refund you."
- **What fails:** It is a good guarantee as an offer. But the page presents this one-week window as the whole remedy. Under New Zealand law, the Consumer Guarantees Act 1993 gives consumers rights on services that can't be contracted out of, and the Fair Trading Act 1986 bans misleading statements about those rights. A reader could take the one-week limit to mean they have fewer rights than the law gives them. Also, "sharper than the day you bought it" is a standard neither side can measure months after the purchase. That makes the guarantee hard to claim against, and it isn't the same test as the promise it backs ("slice a tomato without pressing").
- **Principle:** the jurisdictional claim-posture family. A claim has to be the right kind for the reader's market.
