# Checks

Gate 0 runs first and is binary. The 25 checks run after and produce findings.

Findings, or nothing. There is no composite number. A single figure hides which
check failed, which is the only information this instrument produces.

Every check has the same three lines. **Traces to** names the principle, so the
finding teaches. **Fails when** names the failure. **How to test** is an
instruction you can carry out with the draft in front of you and nothing else —
no brief, no conversation with the writer, no knowledge of what they meant. If a
test seems to need the writer's intent, the draft has failed to supply it, and
that is itself the finding.

## Closing unit

Decide this before any check runs. The closing unit is the piece where the
reader can take the buying or sign-up action directly: a landing page, a sales
email with its own link, an ad that clicks through to checkout. A content
caption whose action lives behind a profile link, or a post whose job is the
save and the share, is upstream of the close and is not the closing unit.

The medium line says which, when it is known. When it does not, decide from
the draft: a price, a checkout, or a direct buy or sign-up action makes it the
closing unit. Say which you decided and why, in one line, before the findings.

When the piece is not the closing unit, O1, O3, O4 and C3 are reported as not
applicable, on one line, and are not findings. They test the close, and the
close is somewhere else. Every other check still runs, and C1 and C2 test
whatever the piece does ask the reader to do.

## Gate 0 — source block

Every statistic, number, date, citation, study, PMID, named person, named
organisation, or named product attribute carries a resolvable source, or the
draft is BLOCKED.

Not a severity. A stop. The failure is silent: a fabricated PMID reads exactly
like a real one.

### What Gate 0 fires on

**Third-party claims about the world.** Efficacy claims. Statistics and
percentages. Studies, trials, citations, PMIDs, journal references. Named
external people and organisations, and anything attributed to them. Claims about
what a substance, ingredient, or method does in a body or in the world. Dates and
events outside the seller's own operation.

### What Gate 0 does not fire on

**First-party commercial terms the seller sets and can verify internally.**
Price. Pack size and servings per pack. The dose of an ingredient in their own
product. Delivery timeframes. Guarantee, refund, and return terms. Stock counts
and deadlines the seller controls.

The reason is the one that grounds the gate: the failure Gate 0 exists to catch
is *silent*. A fabricated PMID reads exactly like a real one, and no reader can
tell. A seller cannot silently fabricate their own price — the number is theirs,
they can check it in a minute, and the buyer finds out at checkout. Blocking a
draft over `NZ$49` treats a thing the seller owns as a thing they must cite, and
it buries the real blocks in noise.

An invented first-party number is not exempt from every test. It is caught
downstream: a dose or a pack size asserted with no support is an unsupported
product claim under P1 and P3. A stock count or a deadline with no reason behind
it is invented urgency under O4.

One fired trigger is enough. Gate 0 does not weigh how many.

## Market

### M1 — awareness stage declared, and the copy addresses it
Traces to: Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), five awareness states.
Fails when: solution-description copy is aimed at problem-aware buyers. Reads as jargon.
How to test: name the stage the copy assumes, then name the stage the reader is
actually in. If you cannot state the second from the draft alone, that is the
finding. A draft that opens with the product's name assumes the reader already
knows the product; a draft that opens with a feeling the reader had this week
assumes only that they had the problem. Say which one this is, and say whether a
first-time visitor would arrive in that state.

### M2 — sophistication stage declared, and the claim form matches it
Traces to: Schwartz, five stages of market sophistication.
Fails when: a bare stage-one claim is made in a stage-four market where every
competitor has already said it, requiring a shift to mechanism.
How to test: take the draft's central claim and ask whether the reader could have
read the same sentence on a competitor's page last week. Superlatives —
"the most advanced", "the best available" — are the shape to look for, because
any seller in any category can assert them. If the claim is one anyone could make
and the draft offers nothing past the claim itself, no mechanism and no new
angle, that is the finding.

### M3 — one reader, named
Traces to: Halbert, write to one person.
Fails when: the copy addresses a committee — "businesses like yours".
How to test: answer, from the draft alone, "who is the one person this was
written to?" in a sentence with a singular human subject. If the only honest
answer names a category, a segment, or an organisation, that is the finding.
Check the second person as well: a "you" that could be a company is not one
reader.

### M4 — desire channeled, not invented
Traces to: Schwartz — copy channels existing desire, it cannot manufacture it.
Fails when: the copy works to make the reader want the category rather than
attaching to a want they arrived with.
How to test: find the earliest sentence that names something the reader already
wanted before they opened the page. If instead the draft has to argue that they
should want it — that it "matters more than ever", that they "deserve" it — the
desire is being built on the page rather than caught, and that is the finding.

## Offer

### O1 — the offer is statable in one sentence
Traces to: Kennedy.
Fails when: the reader cannot say what they get and what it costs.
How to test: **this check is answered by looking for something that may not be
there, so look before you read on.** Write one sentence in this form, using only
words that appear in the draft: "I get ___ for ___." Fill both blanks by
quoting. If you have to invent the price, the quantity, the term, or the delivery
to finish the sentence, that is the finding, and it is the finding even though
the draft never raises the subject and nothing on the page prompts you to ask.
Do not accept the product's name as the first blank or enthusiasm as the second.

### O2 — value equation legible
Traces to: Hormozi, the value equation.
Fails when: none of dream outcome, perceived likelihood, time delay, or effort is
addressed.
How to test: take the four terms one at a time — what the reader ends up with,
why they should believe it will work for them, how long it takes, what it costs
them in effort — and try to quote the draft for each. A term that is merely
asserted, with nothing weighed against it, is not addressed: "you will feel
results in three days" states a time and trades it against nothing. If you cannot
quote a single addressed term, that is the finding.

### O3 — risk reversal present
Traces to: Abraham.
Fails when: all risk sits with the buyer.
How to test: **this check is answered by looking for something that may not be
there.** Ask who is out the money if the product does not work, then find and
quote the sentence that moves that loss to the seller — a guarantee, a refund, a
free trial, a warranty, a pay-on-results term. If no sentence in the draft is
quotable for that, all risk sits with the buyer and that is the finding. The
draft will not mention risk; silence here is exactly what failure looks like, so
do not treat "risk is never discussed" as the check passing.

### O4 — a real reason to act now
Traces to: Kennedy; Cialdini on scarcity.
Fails when: urgency is claimed with no reason behind it, or the draft holds a
real reason to act now and never uses it.
How to test: this check fails in two directions, so report which one you found.
First, locate every urgency claim (a deadline, a stock or capacity count, a
closing date) and for each find the sentence that says why it is true: why the
stock is short, why the date ends the thing, why the cap sits where it does. An
urgency claim with no such sentence is invented. Second, look for a time- or
quantity-bound fact the draft states but never ties to acting now: a limited
batch, a seasonal run, a price that changes, a capped number of places. A reason
the draft already holds and does not use is the finding. A draft with neither an
urgency claim nor such a fact passes this check; the absence of urgency is not
itself a finding. Deadlines and stock counts are the seller's own terms, so the
question is never whether they can be cited, only whether the draft gives a
reason behind them.

## Hook

### H1 — headline carries a specific promise or opens a specific gap
Traces to: Caples, Ogilvy.
Fails when: the headline is a category label — "Introducing our new range".
How to test: read the headline with the rest of the page covered, then state
either the specific result it promises or the specific question it leaves open.
If the most you can state is what kind of thing is for sale, or that something is
new, that is the finding.

### H2 — the first sentence's only job is the second
Traces to: Sugarman, the slippery slide.
Fails when: an opening resolves instead of opening.
How to test: read the first sentence of the body, then stop. Ask what is still
unfinished. A sentence that settles its own subject leaves nothing pulling the
reader into the next one, and that is the finding. A sentence that raises a
timeframe, a condition, or a fact with an obvious missing half does its job.

### H3 — no hook claim the body fails to pay off
Traces to: Hopkins.
Fails when: a promise is made and never redeemed.
How to test: list every claim the draft makes before it explains anything — the
headline, the opening, any bold promise about a result. Then hunt for the later
sentence that pays each one: the how, the exact figure, the evidence, the
qualification. A claim the draft never returns to is the finding. Track each
claim separately; one paid promise does not cover an unpaid one.

### H4 — the medium's scroll-stop physics respected
Traces to: Vault: `parallel-hook-variants-then-narrow-for-scroll-stop-design`,
`single-image-scroll-stop-has-five-sub-300ms-properties`,
`ig-carousel-mistake-myth-hook-states-the-gap-not-the-answer`.
Fails when: a carousel hook is buried on slide 2; a subject line writes a
headline's job.
How to test: name the medium from the draft's own form, then name the first unit
the reader meets in that medium — the H1 and the first screen on a landing page,
slide 1 in a carousel, the subject line in an inbox, the first three seconds of a
video. Check that the hook's work happens inside that first unit. Two failures
count here: the hook arriving after the first unit, and the first unit written in
another medium's form — a page headline behaving like a subject line, a subject
line behaving like a headline. If the draft gives you no way to tell which medium
it is for, say so and test it as a landing page.

## Proof

### P1 — specific over general
Traces to: Hopkins.
Fails when: the copy reaches for the general — "the finest ingredients".
How to test: mark every adjective and every quantity in the draft. For each, ask
whether a seller of a different product in a different category could use the
same word unchanged. "Finest", "advanced", "optimal", "better" survive that swap;
"400mg", "thirty servings", "Thursday" do not. A draft whose claims are all
swappable is the finding. Name the specific that was available and was not used.

### P2 — each argumentative claim traced to evidence
Traces to: Hopkins.
Distinct from Gate 0: that gate catches unsourced facts, this catches unsupported
reasoning.
Fails when: a claim that argues is not traced to anything that supports it.
How to test: separate the sentences that argue from the sentences that describe.
An arguing sentence says something is better, faster, stronger, or more advanced
than an alternative. For each one ask what in the draft makes the argument stand
up — not where the citation is, which is Gate 0's question, but what reasoning is
offered. "The most advanced formula available today" argues and offers nothing
behind it, and that is the finding.

### P3 — mechanism named
Traces to: the Schwartz lineage on unique mechanism.
Fails when: a claim is asserted with no because.
How to test: ask the draft "why does this work?" and answer it in the draft's own
words. The answer has to be a chain — this does that, which produces the result —
not a restatement of the claim in different words. If the strongest answer you
can assemble is the product's name, a brand word, or "it is advanced", no
mechanism is named, and that is the finding.

### P4 — authority attributed, never borrowed
Traces to: Cialdini on authority.
Fails when: the copy says "studies show", "experts agree".
How to test: find every appeal to somebody else's standing and ask "which one?"
A named trial, a named person, a named body, a named institution is attributed.
"Studies", "experts", "doctors", "research", "science" with no name attached is
borrowed, and that is the finding. Borrowing is separate from Gate 0: the gate
blocks the unsourced number, this check names the rhetorical move.

## Voice

### V1 — concrete over abstract
Traces to: Vault: concrete-language-recall.
Fails when: the reader cannot picture it without loading three layers of
vocabulary.
How to test: take each claim sentence and try to see it. "Optimal wellness" gives
you nothing to look at; "your legs are still heavy on Thursday" gives you a body
on a specific day. A draft carried by words that name categories rather than
things is the finding. Point at the sentence and say what it would look like if
the reader could see it.

### V2 — one-to-one address
Traces to: Halbert.
Fails when: the copy uses the corporate plural.
How to test: check who every second-person pronoun refers to and how the
sentences speak. Address aimed at an organisation, a "you" that means a group, or
a draft that talks *about* the reader instead of *to* them is the finding. This
sits next to M3 and is not the same test: M3 asks whether one reader was chosen,
V2 asks whether the sentences reach them.

### V3 — sentence-length variance
Traces to: Sugarman on rhythm.
Fails when: the rhythm is uniform, with no short punch.
How to test: count the words in each sentence of the body and read the sequence
as a series of numbers. A run of similar lengths is the finding. So is a single
sentence that piles clauses on one conjunction — "better and better and harder
and faster" — since it flattens the rhythm from inside one sentence. At least one
sentence should be short enough to land on its own.

### V4 — no AI tells
Traces to: `brain/_system/persona.md`, and the global CLAUDE.md prohibition on
aphoristic closers.
Fails when: the draft carries the em-dash tic, "it's not X, it's Y" closers,
padded tricolons, "in a world where".
How to test: Read `references/ai-tells.md` and check the draft against it.

## Close

### C1 — one CTA
Traces to: Kennedy.
Fails when: three competing asks share the page.
How to test: list every action the draft asks the reader to take. More than one
live ask at the same moment — buy, follow, subscribe, learn more — is the
finding. An instruction that only applies after the purchase, such as how to
claim a refund, is part of the offer and is not a second ask.

### C2 — instructions unambiguous
Traces to: Kennedy.
Fails when: the copy says "learn more" with no statement of what happens next.
How to test: do what the call to action says and then describe the next thing the
reader sees, using only the draft. Where does the click land, what arrives, and
when. If you cannot answer all three from the page, that is the finding. "Learn
more", "get started", and "find out how" fail this unless the draft says what
follows them.

### C3 — response keyed and attributable
Traces to: Hopkins, Kennedy.
Fails when: there is no way to tell this piece produced the response.
How to test: **this check is answered by looking for something that may not be
there.** Ask how the seller would know that *this* piece, rather than any other,
produced a given order or enquiry, then quote the path from the draft. Any
attributable path appropriate to the medium counts: a code the buyer states or
enters, a source-tagged or dedicated link, a phone number used only here, a
reply to an email the buyer actually received, a named landing page. Do not
require a particular tracking product — a quoted code at checkout is a keyed
response and satisfies this check. If nothing in the draft is quotable as a path,
the response is unattributable, and that is the finding. The draft will never
raise the subject, so its silence is the failure rather than a reason to skip.

## Compliance

### X1 — claim posture matches jurisdiction
Traces to: existing brand notes: NZ DSR 1985, US DSHEA, Canadian NPN. X1 and X2
are the two halves of one principle — a claim has to be both the right *kind* for
the jurisdiction and *substantiable* at the strength it is pitched. X1 owns the
kind.
Fails when: a therapeutic claim appears in a supplement context.
How to test: identify the product category from the draft, then collect every
claim about what the product does to a body. Language that treats, cures,
prevents, diagnoses, or eliminates a condition is therapeutic. Language about
supporting or maintaining a normal function is not. A therapeutic verb on a
supplement is the finding, and so is a missing category disclaimer where the
draft's own market requires one.

### X2 — no implied outcome guarantee
Traces to: the jurisdictional claim-posture family X1 belongs to, at the
substantiation end. FTC substantiation doctrine requires a promised outcome to be
one the seller holds competent and reliable scientific evidence for before
making it, not after being asked. Vault:
`msm-compliant-claim-framing-for-nz-performance-supplement`,
`a-supplement-headline-claim-usually-outruns-its-human-evidence`. X2 owns the
strength: a claim can be the right kind for the jurisdiction and still promise
more than the evidence carries.
Fails when: the copy says "you will".
How to test: find every sentence that tells the reader what will happen to them,
then sort it into one of two piles. A promise about what the product does to them
— "you will feel results in three days" — is the finding. A statement about what
the seller will do — "you will get a confirmation email within five minutes",
"the tub arrives inside three working days" — is a fulfilment term, not an
outcome guarantee, and does not fire this check. Hedged effect language, such as
may or is intended to, does not fire it either.
